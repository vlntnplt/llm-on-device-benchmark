For most of human history, time was a strictly local affair. People told the hour by the Sun, and noon was simply the moment when the Sun stood highest in the sky. Because that moment arrives at different instants as one travels east or west, every town effectively kept its own clock. A community might set its public timepiece by a sundial in the market square, and the time shown there could differ by several minutes from a neighbouring town only a short distance away. For a world that moved at the pace of horses and sailing ships, these small discrepancies caused no real trouble. A traveller who arrived an hour out of step with local time had spent days on the road and could simply reset a pocket watch on arrival.

The system of local solar time began to break down in the nineteenth century, and the cause was the railway. Trains moved faster than anything before them, knitting together distant towns into a single network whose operation depended on precise schedules. When each station along a line kept its own local time, building a usable timetable became a nightmare, and the consequences could be deadly. Two trains running on the same track according to clocks that disagreed by a few minutes might find themselves in the same place at the same moment. The railways needed a common reference, and they were powerful enough to impose one.

In Britain, the response was to adopt a single standard across the whole rail network, taken from the time kept at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, on the edge of London. Railway companies began running their trains on Greenwich time regardless of local solar time, distributing the signal along the very telegraph wires that ran beside their tracks. The practice spread until it became the civil time of the entire country, a process sometimes called the coming of railway time. For the first time, a large nation kept one clock, and the ancient link between the hour and the position of the Sun overhead was quietly broken.

The same pressures appeared, on a far larger scale, in North America. The United States and Canada spanned a continent wide enough that solar time varied by hours from one coast to the other, and the railways there faced an even more tangled situation, with many competing companies each keeping its own time standards based on the local time of some chosen city. A passenger crossing the country might pass through dozens of different time standards, and railway stations sometimes displayed a confusing row of clocks. In 1883 the railways of the two countries agreed among themselves to divide the continent into a small number of broad zones, within each of which all clocks would read the same. The zones were spaced roughly an hour apart, so that the time changed by whole hours as one travelled, rather than drifting continuously. This reform was carried out not by governments but by the railway industry acting in concert, and the public, with some grumbling, gradually fell in line.

Distributing an accurate time signal was a problem in its own right, and the solutions of the period were ingenious. Before the telegraph, many port cities relied on a time ball, a large painted sphere mounted on a tower or observatory roof that was hoisted up a mast and then dropped at a fixed hour, often one o'clock. Ships anchored in the harbour watched the ball fall and used the instant to check the chronometers on which their navigation depended. The electric telegraph transformed this practice by allowing a single master clock at an observatory to send a precise signal along the wires to distant stations simultaneously, so that clocks in many cities could be corrected from one authoritative source. Observatories became, in effect, the timekeepers of the nation, selling accurate time to railways, post offices, and the public. This marriage of astronomy and communication technology made it possible, for the first time, to speak meaningfully of the exact same instant in widely separated places.

What remained was to extend the idea around the globe and to fix a starting point from which all zones would be measured. That was the work of an international conference held in Washington in 1884, which gathered delegates from many nations to settle on a single prime meridian, the line of zero longitude. After considerable debate, the conference chose the meridian passing through the Greenwich Observatory, partly because a large share of the world's shipping already used charts based on it. From this line, the surface of the Earth could be divided into a series of standard zones, each fifteen degrees of longitude wide, corresponding to one hour of the Earth's daily rotation. The conference also helped establish the idea of a universal day beginning at midnight at the prime meridian, a reference from which local times everywhere could be derived by adding or subtracting whole hours.

Adoption was gradual and uneven. Some countries embraced standard time quickly; others clung to local time for decades, and a few adopted zones offset by half an hour or even smaller amounts to suit their geography or politics. National borders bent the tidy fifteen-degree bands into irregular shapes, since it was inconvenient for a single country, or even a single city, to be split between two zones. The map of time zones that resulted is therefore a patchwork, following coastlines, frontiers, and administrative boundaries as much as lines of longitude.

A further complication was the seasonal shifting of clocks now widely known as daylight saving time. The idea, promoted in various forms around the turn of the twentieth century, was to move clocks forward in the warmer months so that more daylight fell within ordinary waking hours, in the hope of saving fuel and making better use of the long summer evenings. Several countries adopted the measure during the First World War as an energy-saving step, abandoned it, and then revived it again later. The practice remains contentious, with regular debate over whether the disruption it causes is worth the benefits it is supposed to bring, and the rules differ from country to country and have changed many times.

Underlying all of these civil arrangements is the deeper question of how time itself is defined and measured. For a long time the rotation of the Earth served as the ultimate clock, and the second was defined as a fixed fraction of the day. But the Earth's rotation is not perfectly steady; it varies slightly and is gradually slowing over very long spans. In the middle of the twentieth century, scientists developed atomic clocks, which keep time by counting the extraordinarily regular oscillations associated with atoms of a particular element. These clocks are so stable that they lose or gain less than a second over millions of years, and the official definition of the second was eventually recast in terms of those atomic oscillations rather than the motion of the heavens.

This created a small tension. Atomic time runs at a perfectly even rate, while time based on the Earth's rotation wanders. To keep the two in step, timekeepers occasionally insert an extra second, called a leap second, into the worldwide time standard, so that clocks do not slowly drift away from the rhythm of day and night. The coordinated standard that results is broadcast globally and forms the basis for civil time in every zone, with each local time defined as a whole-number offset from it.

The journey from the sundial in the village square to a network of atomic clocks broadcasting a common signal is, in the end, a story about coordination. As people and information began to move faster, the loose patchwork of local times became a liability, and societies agreed, sometimes reluctantly, to surrender their own noon in exchange for a shared and predictable framework. Today that framework is so woven into daily life that it is almost invisible, governing everything from transport timetables and financial markets to the synchronisation of the computers and communication networks on which the modern world depends.
