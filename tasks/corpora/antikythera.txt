The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek device often described as the world's first known analogue computer. It was recovered in 1901 by sponge divers exploring a Roman-era shipwreck off the small island of Antikythera, which lies between Crete and the Greek mainland. Among the marble and bronze statues hauled up from the seabed was a corroded lump of bronze and wood that few noticed at first. Only when the lump split apart did a researcher spot precisely cut gear teeth inside it, a feature no one expected from an object made around the second century before the common era.

For decades the find resisted explanation, because nothing of comparable mechanical sophistication survives from antiquity. Later studies using X-ray imaging and, more recently, high-resolution computed tomography revealed dozens of interlocking bronze gears housed in a wooden case roughly the size of a shoebox. Inscriptions on its surfaces, a kind of built-in instruction manual, helped scholars reconstruct what the instrument did.

The mechanism modelled the movements of the heavens. By turning a hand crank, a user advanced pointers that tracked the position of the Sun and Moon through the zodiac, displayed the phase of the Moon, and predicted eclipses years in advance. It also kept track of calendars, including the four-year cycle of athletic games such as the Olympics. A delicate train of gears even reproduced the Moon's varying speed across the sky, an irregularity that Greek astronomers had observed but struggled to explain.

What makes the device remarkable is not any single function but the engineering behind it. The precision of its gearing would not be matched in surviving European clockwork for well over a thousand years. The Antikythera mechanism therefore stands as evidence that ancient craftsmen could translate astronomical theory into intricate machinery, and it remains one of the most studied objects from the classical world.
